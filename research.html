Work in Progress

  • Mines, Farms and Forests: Evidence from Brazil (with Ayse-Nihal Yilmaz, Joëlle Noailly, and Steven Poelhekke). Lead author.
    Abstract
    This paper studies the environmental effects of mining activity in Brazil, distinguishing between direct impacts on mining areas and indirect spillover effects on surrounding areas up to 80 kilometres away. We combine two remote-sensing products from MapBiomas covering the period 1985–2024: an annual mining substances raster and a land-use and land-cover classification raster, both aggregated to a regular grid of 10 × 10 kilometre cells across all 27 Brazilian states. Exploiting staggered timing in the onset of mining activity, we apply the Borusyak et al. (2024) imputation estimator to trace the dynamic effect of mining onset on forest cover and farming land use in event time. For directly treated cells we find evidence of forest loss and farming uptake following mining onset. For surrounding cells we document spillover effects in the same direction, with stronger effects observed in the innermost rings. Our results suggest that the aggregate environmental footprint of mining extends well beyond the mine boundary and operates through both direct land conversion and indirect land-use reallocation in the surrounding area.
  • Made in Brazil: Job Creation and Firm Performance Under a Local Content Requirement (with Erik Katovich). Working paper coming soon. Funded by a STEG Small Research Grant.
    Abstract
    Local content requirements (LCRs) are a widely used industrial policy to promote domestic firm growth and job creation, but empirical evidence of their efficacy is thin. We use establishment-level microdata from Brazil to estimate direct and spillover effects of an LCR that required oil companies to source inputs locally. We find that demand shocks channeled to local suppliers by the LCR raise employment, wages, revenues, and profits for these suppliers in the short run and generate positive employment spillovers onto neighboring establishments. Over the long run, local content suppliers exhibit higher employment and wage growth and increase their share of educated and innovative workers relative to controls, but show no improvement in investment, labor productivity, TFP, or export propensity. Local suppliers are also more likely to donate to the political party most supportive of the LCR. Our findings suggest that while LCRs can generate jobs and skill-upgrading, they are less effective at promoting firm performance and may strengthen special interest groups.
  • Local Labor Market Effects of Higher Education Expansion: Evidence from Brazil (with Milena Nakashima and Erik Katovich).
    Abstract
    Brazil’s higher education expansion program (2003–2014) brought federal universities to interior municipalities with little public higher education. We ask whether this local supply shock created skilled jobs or over-education. Linking the Higher Education Census to RAIS for 2001–2021, we estimate doubly-robust staggered difference-in-differences event studies for 204 municipalities that received a first federal university to measure local labor market effects. Over the following decade, formal jobs rise by about 20% and jobs held by college graduates by about 13%. But jobs held by less-educated and over-educated workers grow just as much, suggesting that local labor markets get bigger without becoming more skilled. Gains are concentrated in small municipalities and where public higher education is new, and neighboring municipalities do not seem to benefit from the policy.
